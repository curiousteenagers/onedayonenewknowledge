Prospect Theory

Why does losing money often feel worse than gaining the same amount feels good? Discover Prospect Theory, developed by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, and how people evaluate risks, perceive losses, and make decisions under uncertainty.

Key Concepts

  • The Human Decision Puzzle and Nominal Anchoring: Classical economic models fail during market downturns because individuals facing nominal losses anchor to original purchase prices, setting asking prices twenty-five to thirty-five percent higher than market value.
  • Reference Point Dependence: Rather than evaluating absolute total wealth, human psychology judges financial outcomes relative to a reference point anchor, such as the status quo, past purchase prices, or peer expectations.
  • Loss Aversion and Diminishing Sensitivity: Formulated by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in nineteen seventy-nine, prospect theory proves that the psychological pain of a loss is roughly twice as intense as the pleasure of an equivalent gain.
  • Probability Weighting and Framing Manipulations: Decision makers systematically distort probabilities by overweighting tiny risks while underweighting near-certain outcomes, allowing identical choices to yield opposite behaviors when framed as gains or losses.
  • Asymmetric Risk Preferences and Disposition Effect: Individuals exhibit risk aversion when securing gains but become reckless risk seekers to avoid guaranteed losses, causing investors to sell winning stocks prematurely while holding plummeting assets.

Vocabulary
